07Site Guides — Read Before You Go

History, stories, and context for every major stop. Read on-phone or print these cards to bring with you.

Shuri Castle (首里城)

Day 2 · MONORAIL TO SHURI STATION · ENTRY ~¥400

What it is: Former royal palace of the Ryukyu Kingdom (1429–1879), now a reconstructed fortress and UNESCO World Heritage Site. The castle was largely destroyed in WWII and meticulously rebuilt starting in 1992.

Why visit: Shuri Castle is the symbol of Okinawan identity and sovereignty. Unlike Japanese castles on the mainland, it reflects a unique Ryukyuan architectural style influenced by Chinese design. The palace is not a feudal lord's residence but the center of royal government and ritual.

What to see: The Shureimon Gate (Unesco-registered), the main palace buildings with red walls, the Hōkoku (royal treasure) room, gardens, and views over Naha. The interior shows how the royal court lived and governed.

History deep-dive: The Ryukyu Kingdom was independent for 450 years, maintaining trade with China, Japan, Southeast Asia, and Europe. Shuri Castle was at the heart of this cosmopolitan island nation. When Japan annexed Ryukyu in 1879, the royal family was exiled. In 1945, the castle was burned during the Battle of Okinawa. Reconstruction took 15 years (1992–2007) and is considered Japan's most extensive historical restoration project.

Tip: Arrive 8:30am when it opens to beat tour groups. Allow 1–2 hours inside.

Tsuboya Pottery District (つぼや)

Day 2 · WALK OR 15-MIN BUS FROM SHURI · FREE TO EXPLORE

What it is: A working pottery district with kilns, studios, and small shops lining narrow lanes. Tsuboya has been Okinawa's ceramic center since the 1600s.

Why visit: Unlike tourist-ified craft villages, Tsuboya is a genuine artisan community. Potters still work here daily, and you'll see kilns smoking and clay being shaped. It's a living history, not a museum.

What to see: Pottery studios with open doors, the Tsuboya Pottery Museum (¥315), small galleries, tea shops, and restaurants. The lanes are narrow and atmospheric — perfect for photography and wandering.

History: Okinawa's pottery tradition dates back thousands of years, but Tsuboya was formalized as a potter's guild in the 1600s when the Ryukyu Kingdom consolidated ceramic production. The distinctive red and brown earthenware you see here served everyday Okinawan life for centuries. After WWII, Tsuboya almost disappeared as traditional crafts fell out of fashion, but a revival movement in the 1970s brought it back to life.

Tip: Some studios offer short pottery-making sessions (¥2,000–3,000, reserve ahead). Pottery here is reasonably priced and makes authentic gifts (no shipping hassles like heavier items).

Kerama Islands Diving & Humpback Whales

Days 3, 4, 5 · TOMARI PORT DEPARTURE ~7:30–8AM · ~¥18,000–25,000/DAY

What it is: A small archipelago (~1hr boat from Naha) with pristine coral reefs, multiple dive sites, and winter humpback whale migration routes. Kerama is a Quasi-National Marine Park.

Why dive here: Kerama offers three distinct ecosystems: shallow reefs (10–15m) for beginners/snorkelers, deeper walls (25–40m) with larger marine life, and shipwrecks. Water visibility is 20–40m, excellent for March. Corals here are healthier than many Southeast Asian reefs.

Humpback whales (Jan–Mar): Female humpbacks migrate south from Alaska to breed and calve in Okinawan waters. January–March is peak season. Whales are often spotted en route to the dive sites (boat captains will call out sightings). You may hear their songs underwater — a haunting, otherworldly experience. NOTE: Whale encounters are not guaranteed, but sightings are common in March.

Dive sites for 3 days: Request variety — shallow reefs on Day 3, deeper walls on Day 4, different site on Day 5. Mix of coral gardens, underwater sculptures, and fish-rich channels.

5mm wetsuit required (March water: ~19–22°C). Bring motion-sickness medication if prone — 1-hour boat ride can be rough.

Tip: Book all three dive days with the same operator to ensure consistency. Aloha Divers Okinawa, SEAKERS, and GOLD Scuba all run daily Kerama trips with English guides.

Yanbaru National Park & Yanbaru Kuina

Day 6 (Campervan) · NORTHERN OKINAWA · FREE ENTRY (Trails ~¥300–500 Fees)

What it is: Japan's largest subtropical evergreen forest, established as a national park in 2016. A UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of Asia's most biodiverse ecosystems.

Why visit: Yanbaru is raw, wild nature — unlike manicured tourist sites. The forest is impenetrably dense, the air humid, the sounds alive with endemic bird calls. It feels prehistoric.

The Yanbaru Kuina (Okinawa Rail): This tiny, flightless bird exists nowhere else on Earth. Discovered in 1981, it's been Okinawa's symbol ever since. Fewer than a few thousand remain — classified as critically endangered. The bird is shy, runs instead of flies, and has adapted to life on a single island over millennia. Seeing one is genuinely rare (odds ~10–20% on a dedicated tour, nearly 0% on casual walks). Early morning or dusk tours, or a night safari, give the best chances.

Trails: Daisekirinzan Forest (easy, 1km paved walkthrough rocky formations), Hiji Waterfall (1.5km, ¥300), Yona Dake summit (challenging 6km, 4 hours). All run through dense subtropical jungle.

Flora: Banyan trees, endemic orchids, ancient ferns. The forest is a living botanical garden.

Tip: Wear proper hiking shoes (trails are muddy). Bring insect repellent and water. Campervan gives you flexibility to spend 6–8 hours here; day-trippers from Naha often feel rushed. Sleep at a rest area or beach parking near Nago.

Sefa-Utaki (斎場御嶽)

Day 7 (Campervan) · SOUTHERN OKINAWA · ENTRY ¥200 · GUIDED TOUR ¥300

What it is: Okinawa's most sacred site and the holiest shrine in the indigenous Ryukyuan religion. A natural rock formation on a forested hillside overlooking the sea, designated as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Religious significance: In Okinawan belief, "utaki" are natural places where deities and ancestral spirits descend to Earth. Sefa-Utaki was the supreme utaki — the spiritual heart of the Ryukyu Kingdom for 450 years.

Women's spirituality: Unlike patriarchal Japanese Shinto, Okinawan religion was led entirely by women. The highest priestess was called the Kikoe-Ōkimi (Esteemed Lady Who Listens). Until the 20th century, no men except the king were allowed to enter Sefa-Utaki — and the king had to dress as a woman to enter. This site was exclusively women's sacred space.

The sanctuary: Walking through the forest, you pass several prayer points connected by stone paths. The innermost chamber, called "Sangui," is a triangular tunnel formed by two colossal rocks leaning together. Inside, you look out through the stone gateway toward Kudaka Island, believed to be the birthplace of the Ryukyuan people, where the creator deity Amamikiyo first descended from heaven.

WWII damage: The site was heavily damaged in 1945 (Battle of Okinawa); the limestone overhangs were much larger before shelling destroyed them. Decades of forest regrowth have slowly healed the scars.

Tip: Get an English-language brochure at entry. Walking the trail takes ~30–45 min. The site is still an active place of worship — you may encounter locals praying. Show respect and quiet reverence. The Sangui chamber is powerful; sit in silence for a moment.

Emi no Mise & Ogimi Village — Longevity Culture

Day 7 (Campervan) · NORTHERN OKINAWA · LONGEVITY MEAL ¥1,575 (RESERVE AHEAD: 0980-44-3220)

What it is: Ogimi Village is one of the world's five "Blue Zones" — regions where people routinely live past 100 in good health. Emi no Mise is a farm-to-table restaurant serving the "longevity meal" that researchers believe contributes to this phenomenon.

Why visit: This is not a tourist trap; it's a genuine window into how Okinawans live. Emi herself (age 80s+) grows vegetables in her garden and cooks traditional dishes passed down from her grandmother. Scientists have literally studied this meal to understand longevity.

The Longevity Meal (Choju-zen): ~15 dishes featuring local Okinawan vegetables: goya (bitter melon), ingana (dock leaves), kandaba (taro leaves), handama (purple sweet potato), shikwasa (citrus), papaya greens. Each vegetable is nutrient-dense and full of antioxidants. The meal includes simmered, stir-fried, and raw preparations to preserve vitamins. Emi uses minimal salt, fat, and sugar — the opposite of the Western diet.

The science: Researchers from the Blue Zones project have attributed Okinawan longevity to: (1) high-vegetable, low-meat diet, (2) active daily life (gardening, walking), (3) strong community bonds (seniors gather daily), (4) spiritual practice. Shikwasa (Okinawan lime) contains nobiletin, a compound that inhibits cancer cell development.

Ogimi itself: Rolling hills, dense vegetation, quiet roads. The village is 80% forest. Elders can be seen gardening and playing gateball (Japanese croquet). It's a vision of aging that feels radically different from Western retirement homes.

Tip: Reserve Choju-zen meal by 4pm the day before (tel 0980-44-3220). Hours: 11:30am–5pm. Closed Tue/Wed. Emi also sells homemade shikwasa ponzu vinegar as a souvenir (¥840). The meal is an experience, not just a photo — come hungry and ready to taste vegetables you've never had before.

Okinawa Prefectural Peace Memorial Park

Day 8 (Bike) · SOUTHERN OKINAWA · FREE ENTRY · MUSEUM ¥300

What it is: A museum and memorial complex dedicated to the Battle of Okinawa, the final major battle of WWII in the Pacific (March–June 1945). One of the deadliest single-island campaigns in history.

The battle: US forces invaded Okinawa with ~180,000 troops. Japanese defenders numbered ~100,000, plus ~100,000 Okinawan civilians conscripted as laborers and militia. After 82 days of intense fighting, the Japanese surrendered. Casualties: ~49,000 US, ~110,000 Japanese military, ~100,000 Okinawan civilians. The final weeks were apocalyptic — Japanese soldiers ordered mass civilian suicides rather than surrender; mothers threw children from cliffs.

Why visit: Peace Memorial Park is not a tourist attraction; it's a somber, essential historical record. The museum documents this tragedy through photographs, letters, artifacts, and survivor testimonies. It's difficult, heavy, and important.

The site: The main feature is the "Mabuni" (Rock of Intense Fighting), a cliff face overlooking the sea where the final battle took place. The memorial lists the names of all ~200,000+ people who died in the battle, regardless of nationality or side. The "Cornerstone of Peace" monument carries the inscription: "Let all souls rest in peace, for we shall never repeat this mistake."

Tip: Spend at least 1–2 hours here. The museum audio in English provides crucial context. The Himeyuri Peace Museum (dedicated to female student-nurses who perished) is nearby if you want deeper history. Many Okinawans consider this site essential to understanding the island's identity and why Okinawa is deeply skeptical of military presence today.

Gyokusendo Cave (玉泉洞) & Okinawa World

Day 8 (Bike) · SOUTHERN OKINAWA · ENTRY ~¥2,000

What it is: A massive limestone cavern 5km long, with 850m open to the public via comfortable walkways. One of Japan's longest caves and the second-longest in the country. Formed over 300,000 years by underground streams.

Geology: The cave walls are covered in stalactites and stalagmites in shades of orange, red, white, and cream — colors created by mineral-rich water dripping over millennia. The formations are genuinely spectacular and feel otherworldly.

Okinawa World complex: The cave is part of a larger theme park (Okinawa World) that includes a traditional craft village ("Ryukyu Kingdom Castle Town"), a snake museum, and a performing arts stage. The cave itself is the main event; the rest is optional.

Why visit: The cave is a geological marvel. Walking through it, you get a visceral sense of deep time — 300,000 years of patient water carving rock. In Japanese culture, caves are sacred spaces (kami dwelling places); this one has that feeling.

Practical: The cave is cool (~21°C year-round), humid, and dimly lit by strategically placed lighting. The walkway is safe but has some stairs. Take your time — rush clients miss the beauty. Wear non-slippery shoes (cave floor is slick from moisture and algae).

Tip: After the cave, grab lunch at the Okinawa World restaurants (overpriced but convenient). The cave takes 30–45min to walk through at a normal pace. Total time including shops/parking: 2–3 hours.

American Village (アメリカンビレッジ)

Day 9 · CHATAN (CENTRAL OKINAWA) · FREE ENTRY · SHOPPING & DINING

What it is: A commercial complex themed around American Americana. Think Route 66, retro diners, beach culture kitsch — transplanted to Okinawa's coast. It's part shopping mall, part theme park, part beach hangout.

Why visit: American Village is emblematic of Okinawa's unique postwar identity. The island hosted massive US military bases after WWII, and American cultural influence is woven into local life in strange, fascinating ways. American Village is where that collision of cultures becomes most visible and commercialized.

What to see: Vintage Americana decor (chrome diners, neon signs, classic cars), sunset views over the sea, souvenir shops, clothing brands (Abercrombie, Gap), food courts with both Japanese and American fast-casual options. There's a Ferris wheel with sea views.

Sunset timing: American Village is most magical at sunset. The sky turns orange, the neon lights up, and the sea breeze carries that nostalgic, liminal atmosphere. Many Okinawans come here on dates or evenings.

Reality check: American Village is touristy and commercial. It's not "authentic" Okinawa, but it's an authentic expression of Okinawa's cultural synthesis — East meets West, tradition meets kitsch. Worth 1–2 hours to wander and feel the vibe.

Tip: Arrive 4–5pm, stay through sunset (~6pm in March), grab dinner at one of the restaurants. Parking is easy. Photo ops everywhere.

Makishi Public Market & Fish Markets

Day 9 · CENTRAL NAHA · FREE TO BROWSE · MEALS ~¥1,500–3,000

What it is: An energetic public market (shotengai) where locals buy fresh fish, produce, meat, and prepared foods. It's a working market, not a tourist attraction, though visitors are welcome.

Why visit: This is where ordinary Okinawan food culture happens. Fishmongers shout over each other, vendors prepare sashimi to order, grandmothers buy vegetables, and the air smells like the sea. It's authentic, chaotic, and alive.

What to eat: Goya champuru (stir-fried bitter melon), mubushi (grilled fish), raw fish sashimi (extremely fresh, ~¥2,000–3,000 for a lunch set), sea urchin (uni), local seaweed snacks. The 2nd floor of Makishi has prepared-food stalls where vendors cook to order.

Okinawan seafood: Unlike mainland Japan, Okinawa's seafood reflects tropical waters. You'll see varieties unfamiliar to Tokyo markets: coral trout, spiny lobster, sea cucumber, small fish for drying. The region's fishing traditions go back millennia.

Tip: Go early (8–11am) when it's busiest and freshest. Bring cash (many vendors are cash-only). Point and smile — language barriers dissolve when you're interested in food. A vendor might hand you a taste of something; accept graciously. Markets are the best place to encounter real Okinawan people and food without filters.

Ricoland & Motorcycle Shops

Day 9 · VARIOUS LOCATIONS IN NAHA · OPEN 10AM–7PM

What it is: Ricoland is Okinawa's main motorcycle apparel chain, stocking helmets, riding gear, boots, gloves, and brand-name motorcycle clothing (Alpinestars, Fox, etc.).

Why visit (if you care about bikes): If you're a motorcyclist, it's a natural stop to browse gear, grab a souvenir riding shirt, or pick up last-minute supplies (chain lube, tire cleaner, socks). The staff are bike-enthusiasts and can chat about riding Okinawa if you want.

Context: Motorcycles are deeply embedded in Okinawan youth culture. Scooter culture is huge; big-bike tourism is growing. Ricoland serves both. Prices are comparable to Singapore but selection is more varied (Japanese brands emphasized).

Tip: Browse for 30–60 min if you're a gearhead; skip if you're not. Good place to grab a Ricoland t-shirt as a souvenir for friends back home.

Seaside Drive-In & Beachside Dining

Day 9 · VARIOUS COASTAL LOCATIONS · CASUAL DINING ~¥2,000–4,000

What it is: Open-air or semi-covered beach restaurants where you dine with sea views, often with sand underfoot or a few meters away. They range from casual food trucks to sit-down establishments.

Why visit: Dining by the sea while watching the sky change colors is one of the simple pleasures of Okinawa. The food is usually seafood-focused and unpretentious.

What to eat: Grilled fish, tacos (Okinawan-American fusion), shave ice, sashimi bowls, local beer. The menu is often hand-scrawled and changes based on what the fishermen brought in that morning.

Timing: Go at sunset (late afternoon in March, ~5–6pm). The light is perfect, the breeze picks up off the water, and you'll share the space with locals doing the same thing.

Tip: Most drive-ins are casual and first-come-first-served. No reservations. Bring cash. Expect to spend 1–2 hours lounging, not rushing. This is the Okinawan pace of life — slow, social, tied to the sea.
